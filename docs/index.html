The problem

Flight delays cost the U.S. an estimated $32.9 billion a year, about half of it borne by passengers. If an airline knew an hour ahead which departures were likely to run late, it could re-plan crews and gates, and passengers could re-book early.

A flight counts as delayed when it leaves 15 minutes or more after schedule, the U.S. Department of Transportation's definition.

How it works

Every departure is described by 22 facts known before take-off: the schedule, the airline and route, the aircraft, the weather at the airport that hour, how busy the airport is, and how delayed flights were there two to three hours earlier.

An XGBoost model of 400 decision trees turns those facts into a probability. It was trained on days 1–17 of each month, tuned on days 18–21, and tested on days 22–31, so every score here comes from flights it never saw.

What we found

Delays build through the day: about 6% of 5 a.m. departures leave late, against 38% at 9 p.m. The strongest signals are the departure hour and recent delays at the airport, then the airline. Weather matters less once you know how the airport is running.

The model catches most delays but raises false alarms too, so it suits early warnings rather than automatic decisions.
